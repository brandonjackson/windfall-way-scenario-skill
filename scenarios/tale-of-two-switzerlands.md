# A Tale of Two Switzerlands

## A Change in Character

For several years AI behaved like a normal technology. Around December 2027, however, the character of the economy began to visibly change. Frontier models could now reliably perform four-hour computer-based tasks, which meant they could complete work bundles end-to-end. They had, in other words, become good enough for firms to reorganise skilled white-collar work around them. Moreover, the pace of capability improvements showed no sign of slowing down.

## Switzerland Strong as Ever

At first, Switzerland seemed to be managing the transition smoothly. Global uncertainty around AI-induced upheaval pushed capital into the country and put upward pressure on the franc. GDP growth had increased, the franc was stronger than ever, and Switzerland's flagship sectors looked more productive than ever.

## Fissures Emerging

But soon enough fissures started to emerge.

Swiss banks had benefited from sustained wealth-management inflows. At the same time, AI had become effective at much of the work done by analysts, compliance officers, and administrative staff. Most headcount reduction came through natural attrition driven by baby boomers reaching the pension age, smaller junior intakes, and simple non-replacement of previous positions. Banks and insurers increased revenue per FTE, while employment in the sector fell.

Leading precision-manufacturing firms also did well in the wake of a global robotics revolution. But many export-oriented firms had no choice but to automate aggressively in order to remain competitive under a strong franc. Premium-goods producers preserved human craft where it protected brand value, while seeking to automate much else.

Increased returns to capital benefited asset owners. Older homeowners and households with material financial assets gained. But much of this wealth was tied up in real estate and (future) pensions, and did not translate into broad-based demand for labour. Household consumption softened among younger renters, recent graduates, and displaced clerical workers.

## A Bifurcated Labor Market

Across the country, low- to medium-qualification white-collar workers, especially in kaufmännische Berufe, saw employment contract. For many clerical functions, lower unit costs did not generate more demand. Firms still needed only so much bookkeeping, routine HR processing, internal reporting, and compliance work. The main effect of automation was to reduce the labour needed per unit of output. Given their exposure to global competition many firms used the savings to accelerate further automation rather than expand hiring.

The labour market became bifurcated. Government employees, care workers, skilled tradespeople, and some highly qualified professionals remained sheltered. Clerical and junior white-collar workers did not.

Youth unemployment among graduates of commercial apprenticeships increased. The kaufmännische Lehre came under pressure because firms no longer knew which clerical tasks would still exist by the time a cohort graduated.

Demographic challenges did not disappear. Baby boomers kept entering retirement and birth rates remained at an all-time low. That combination further increased firms' incentive to automate where they could. At the same time Switzerland still needed nurses, care workers, childcare staff, construction workers, hospitality workers, and skilled tradespeople. But these shortages did not automatically absorb displaced workers from other sectors. Many vacancies were in credentialed, shift-based, physically demanding roles and would often require workers to move or accept long commutes. Reallocation happened, but slowly and unevenly.

Wage-linked tax receipts weakened just as spending on unemployment insurance and retraining rose. The impact across the country was uneven: stronger Cantons were asked to support weaker ones through the National Fiscal Equalization mechanism just as their own revenues were coming under pressure. AHV contributions also dipped just as the number of pensioners was steadily increasing.

## "Something Must Be Done"

By summer 2030, as the 2031 parliamentary elections came into view, two popular initiatives were in motion.

The more advanced initiative, the Swiss Work and Sovereignty Initiative, came from an unlikely coalition: environmentalists alarmed by the energy consumption of frontier AI, populist-right campaigners arguing that foreign models were corroding Swiss culture and opportunity, and angry that Swiss firms had cut positions while buying overseas AI services.

The initiative sought to restrict the use of foreign frontier models for sensitive applications; privilege Swiss- or European-hosted alternatives; and impose a levy on foreign model use to fund apprenticeship guarantees. Exporters, pharma firms, and banks warned that such restrictions would damage competitiveness.

A second initiative, still gathering signatures, demanded a right to human work in designated sectors. It required minimum human staffing or mandatory human review for specified functions. Firms receiving public contracts would have to commit to maintaining apprenticeship places at or above their 2026 baseline. The initiative drew support not only from unions, but also from professional associations and training bodies that saw it as a way to defend standards, apprenticeship pathways, and occupational status.

The Federal Council now faced a deadline and had to decide how to respond to popular discontent.
