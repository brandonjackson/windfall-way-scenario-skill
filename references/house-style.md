# House Style

Reverse-engineered from nine scenarios across multiple workshops and
horizons. Brandon should review and correct — especially "What Makes a
Scenario Good."

## Voice and Tense

Past tense, third person omniscient. The narrator writes as a historian
looking back on recent events from inside the scenario's timeframe. No
hedging — the scenario presents its world as fact. There's no "might" or
"could" — that tentativeness is left for the workshop discussion, not
the text.

## Policy Off

Windfall scenarios are **policy off** by default. The world moves because
of technology, markets, firms, households and ordinary institutional
inertia, not because a government designed a response. The policy
response is what the workshop exists to produce, so a scenario that
already contains one leaves the room grading someone else's homework
instead of doing its own.

In practice:

- **No invented policy as the engine.** No named Act, registry, levy,
  programme, waiver or reform drives the causal chain. If you took the
  policy out and the story collapsed, the scenario is policy on.
- **Government appears, but reacting and constrained.** It can be slow,
  outpaced, stretched, divided, stalling or firefighting: "could not
  afford to slow down", "faced a deadline and had to decide". Existing
  rules and institutions already on the books today are fine as setting.
- **End before the decision.** The closing bind is the choice the state
  and society now face, not the aftermath of a choice already made.

The corpus shows this: Crossing the Threshold, Open Frontier, Paper
Prosperity and A Tale of Two Switzerlands all end with government facing
the bind, not having answered it. (The Great Pushback, a Discipline
scenario from another team, is the exception, and even there the change
is a broad societal backlash, not one instrument.)

Override only when the brief says explicitly that the commissioner wants
a specific policy tested. Record that in `BRIEF.md`.

## Altitude: the Social Contract

Windfall scenarios sit at the level of the **social contract**: the
implicit deal between citizens, workers, firms and the state about work,
security, fairness, trust and who pays. They are not case studies of one
sector, system or instrument.

A useful test: can you state the scenario's shift as a change in *who
owes what to whom*? "Work stops being the route to security", "the state
loses its role as the guarantor of trust", "the young are asked to fund a
settlement they were locked out of". If the one-line summary is instead
about a mechanism (a registry, a credit model, a permit system, a tax
rate), the altitude is too low.

Specific issues the commissioner raises (a security fear, a sector, a
technology) are **lenses and texture**, not the spine. Let them appear
as vivid instances of the larger shift, one or two sentences each,
never as the thing the whole story is about.

## Horizon and Register

The planning horizon (H1/H2/H3) shapes the register of the scenario —
how familiar or transformed the world feels — but is independent of
length and narrative structure. An H3 scenario can be 500 words of
compressed prose; an H2 scenario can be 900 words of institutional
detail. The horizon determines what kind of world you're writing about;
the length and structure are determined by the audience and workshop
format.

The existing corpus is mostly H2 (Crossing the Threshold, Transformation
Economy, Open Frontier, Paper Prosperity, Tale of Two Switzerlands —
all set in strained but recognisable institutions). The SF booklet
scenarios are H3 (The Post-Work Society reimagines the social contract
entirely). Both registers follow the same house style principles below.

## Length and Structure

Scenarios range from ~300 words (Paper Prosperity — compact, essayistic)
to ~900 words (Tale of Two Switzerlands — more institutional detail for
a policy-literate audience). The typical UK workshop scenario runs
550–750 words.

Two structural patterns appear across the corpus:

**Sectioned narrative** (most common): a title, an opening paragraph
that sets the thesis, then 4–6 short sections with bold phrase headers.
Each section advances one link in the causal chain. Sections are 1–2
paragraphs. Examples: Crossing the Threshold, Transformation Economy,
Open Frontier.

**Continuous prose** (shorter scenarios): no section headers, reads as
a single argument. Example: Paper Prosperity.

Both patterns share the same skeleton: a cascading causal chain where
each beat depends on the last.

### Title conventions

Title includes location and timeframe when relevant:
- "UK 2030: Crossing the Threshold"
- "USA 2030: Paper Prosperity"
- Or a standalone evocative title: "A Tale of Two Switzerlands"

### Preamble

Some scenarios (Transformation Economy, Open Frontier) carry a preamble
block — a short summary paragraph plus provenance note (e.g. "Scenario 4
from the GO-Science AI 2030 scenarios"). This sits above the narrative
and is distinct from it. Include when the scenario is commissioned by
or adapted from another programme's framework.

## Level of Specificity

Concrete but selective. The scenarios use:

- **Specific dates**: "December 2027", "Q4 2028", "summer 2030"
- **Specific numbers**: "4% GDP", "17% unemployment", "80% market share"
- **Real institutions**: OBR, HMRC, NHS, Lloyd's of London, AHV,
  kaufmännische Lehre — but no named politicians or fictional individuals
- **Vivid compressed detail**: "AI firm CEOs smiling", "didn't eat out,
  go shopping (or pay taxes)", "click approve twice"

The level of institutional specificity adapts to the audience. The UK
scenarios reference HMRC and OBR; the Swiss scenario names Cantons,
AHV, and kaufmännische Berufe. The scenario speaks the audience's
institutional language.

## Tone

Journalistic with literary touches. Short, punchy sentences mixed with
longer analytical ones. Deliberately provocative but not polemical —
it shows rather than argues. Compressed, almost headline-quality
phrases: "Abundant intelligence had permanently devalued expertise."

The writing can be wry: "Capability increases in AI speechwriting
proved insufficient to hide the growing contradictions of the
transformation economy." It can be vivid: "The underwriters at Lloyd's
of London quietly exited the cyber insurance market on Monday morning."

Avoids:
- Academic register (no "moreover", "it should be noted")
- Policy paper register (no "we recommend", "stakeholders should consider")
- Science fiction register (no world-building for its own sake)
- Moralising (no "this was wrong" or "leaders should have acted")

## Handling Uncertainty

The scenario never breaks frame. It doesn't say "in this scenario..." or
"one possible future is..." — it tells you what happened. The uncertainty
is structural: participants know this is a scenario because of the
workshop context, not because the text reminds them.

Exception: preamble blocks can state the scenario's position in a
framework ("This scenario describes one possible future from the
Hollow Boom quadrant").

## Opening Moves

Several scenarios share an opening move: AI starts as a normal
technology, then something shifts. This "crossing the threshold"
structure appears in at least three of the five examples. It works
because it meets a sceptical audience where they are before pulling
them into unfamiliar territory.

Other openings lead with the macro picture (Paper Prosperity: "America
looked better than ever—on paper") and let the dissonance emerge.

## Closing Moves

Every scenario ends on an unresolved tension — the thing the workshop
exists to discuss. The pattern is a compressed articulation of the bind:

- "Being highly automated, asset rich, and fiscally poor."
- "The government could not afford to slow down. And it could not
  afford to keep going."
- "The Federal Council now faced a deadline and had to decide how to
  respond to popular discontent."
- "They just wanted good jobs that gave their kids a shot at the
  American Dream."

The closing never resolves. It hands the dilemma to the room.

## Recurring Motifs

Certain phrases and concepts recur across scenarios as a shared
vocabulary:

- "Abundant intelligence had permanently devalued expertise"
- The fiscal bind: labour/consumption taxed, not capital
- Top 20% of earners paying 75% of income tax
- Digital replacements that "don't eat out, go shopping, or pay taxes"
- The career ladder with vanished middle rungs

These create continuity across a scenario programme. Reuse where it
serves the audience; vary when writing for a new context.

## What Makes a Scenario Good

- **Cascading logic**: each section causes the next. The reader can
  trace the chain of consequences.
- **A landing tension**: the scenario ends on a genuine dilemma, not a
  conclusion. Something the room must discuss.
- **Productive discomfort**: makes participants uneasy in a way that
  connects to their real responsibilities.
- **Audience-native language**: speaks in the institutions and vocabulary
  the participants actually use (HMRC for UK civil servants, Cantons
  for Swiss policymakers).
- **Concision**: short enough to read aloud in 3–5 minutes. Every
  sentence earns its place.
- **Plausible provocation**: specific enough to feel real, provocative
  enough to challenge assumptions.
- **Emotional resonance**: "People who had followed the rules, earned
  degrees and built careers now found themselves competing for gig work."
  The best lines connect macro dynamics to lived experience.

## Anti-patterns

- Scenarios that read like policy papers (arguing for a position)
- Scenarios that resolve their own tension (no need for a workshop)
- Scenarios so abstract they don't land emotionally
- Scenarios that are obviously implausible and invite "that would never
  happen" derailment
- Scenarios too long to read and absorb in a workshop setting
- Scenarios that moralise rather than show
- Breaking frame with "in this scenario" or "imagine if"
- Policy-on scenarios: an invented Act, programme or reform drives the
  story, and the room is handed a policy to grade rather than a bind to
  resolve
- Scenarios pitched too low: the story is about one system, sector or
  instrument (a registry, a model update, a tax) rather than the social
  contract it strains
