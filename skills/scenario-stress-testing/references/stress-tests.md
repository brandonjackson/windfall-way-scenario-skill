# Scenario Stress Tests

Structured questions to apply to a draft scenario before tuning.
Used by the `scenario-stress-testing` skill, which works through the
categories, then the judges, then simulates the workshop conversation,
and records severity-ranked findings that `scenario-drafting` revises
against.

---

## Policy Stance and Altitude

- Is the scenario policy off? Does any invented Act, programme, registry,
  levy or reform drive the causal chain? Remove it in your head: does
  the story still stand? If not, the scenario is policy on.
- Does government appear only as it would absent a designed response:
  slow, reacting, constrained, facing the choice?
- Does the ending hand the room a decision still to be made, rather than
  the fallout of one already taken?
- Can the scenario's shift be stated as a change in the social contract
  (who owes what to whom)? Or is it really a story about one system,
  sector or instrument?
- Are the commissioner's specific concerns present as instances of the
  larger shift, or has one of them become the whole story?

Unless `BRIEF.md` explicitly asks for a policy to be tested, a
policy-driven causal chain or a single-instrument story is **Blocking**:
it pre-empts the room's work.

## Internal Consistency

- Map the key economic arguments in the scenario. Are they all
  internally consistent? Does A actually lead to B?
- Are there any causal claims that contradict each other?
- Do the numbers add up? (e.g. if unemployment is X%, do the fiscal
  implications match?)
- Are there implicit assumptions that haven't been stated? Could they
  undermine the logic if surfaced?

## Audience Resonance

- Would this scenario resonate with the specific audience in the brief?
- Does it speak in their institutional language?
- Does it connect to decisions they actually face, or does it float
  above their responsibilities?
- Is there a risk that participants dismiss it as irrelevant to their
  context?

## Plausibility

- Where is the scenario most vulnerable to "that would never happen"
  objections?
- Are those vulnerabilities fatal (would derail the workshop) or
  acceptable (can be acknowledged and moved past)?
- Are the timelines credible? Could the changes described plausibly
  unfold in the time given?
- Does the scenario rely on any single point of failure that a
  sceptic would immediately target?

## Productive Tension

- What is the central dilemma? Can you state it in one sentence?
- Does the scenario force a genuine trade-off, or does it have an
  obvious "right answer"?
- Are there at least two defensible positions a participant could
  take in response?
- Does the ending hand the dilemma to the room, or does it resolve
  prematurely?

## Emotional Register

- Does the scenario land emotionally or does it read as abstract?
- Is there at least one moment that connects macro dynamics to lived
  experience?
- Is the tone appropriate for the audience? (Too provocative risks
  defensiveness; too measured risks boredom.)

## Coverage and Gaps

- What dimensions of the world does the scenario cover? What does it
  leave out?
- Are the omissions deliberate (keeping it focused) or accidental
  (blind spots)?
- Does the scenario over-index on one causal mechanism at the expense
  of others?

## Workshop Dynamics

- What is the most likely unproductive conversation this scenario
  will trigger?
- Are there any terms, claims, or framings that will distract from
  the core dilemma?
- Is the scenario short enough to read aloud and absorb in the
  workshop setting?
- Does it give participants enough to work with, without
  over-specifying their response?

## Judges

Four adversarial readers, each stress-testing the scenario from a
different standpoint. Run each judge after the category questions
above — they overlap deliberately, because the same flaw looks
different depending on who it bothers.

### The Economist

*Cares about: internal consistency of the economic logic.*

- Trace every causal claim in the scenario. Does each link actually
  follow from the last, or is there a gap papered over with
  confident prose?
- Are the macro and micro stories consistent? (If GDP is growing,
  who is spending? If unemployment is high, who is producing?)
- Would a competent economist accept the fiscal arithmetic, or
  would they dismiss the scenario before the workshop conversation
  even starts?
- Does the scenario confuse a one-time level shift with a growth
  rate change? A stock with a flow? A nominal figure with a real one?
- Are the timelines for economic adjustment plausible? Markets,
  labour, and institutions move at different speeds — does the
  scenario respect that?

### The Citizen

*Cares about: standards of living and the social contract.*

- Can an ordinary person see themselves in this world? Is there at
  least one moment where macro dynamics land as lived experience?
- What happens to the median household, not the mean? Does the
  scenario show who gains and who loses, or does it hide
  distributional pain behind aggregate numbers?
- Is the social contract legible? Can a reader tell what the
  implicit deal between citizen and state is in this world — and
  where it's fraying?
- Does the scenario acknowledge the things people actually care
  about — housing, health, education, security, dignity at work —
  or does it stay at the level of GDP and productivity?
- Would a non-expert read this and feel provoked to discuss, or
  would they feel talked past?

### The Policymaker

*Cares about: whether the scenario feels serious enough to work with.*

- Would a senior official take this into a meeting, or would they
  worry it makes them look naive?
- Are the institutional references accurate and current? Wrong
  details — a misnamed body, an outdated process — erode
  credibility fast.
- Does the scenario present a problem a government could actually
  face, or is it a thought experiment with no policy surface?
- Are the policy levers implied by the scenario real and
  recognisable? Would a policymaker see options they could actually
  pull, even if the choices are hard? Implied, not pulled: if the
  scenario pulls the lever itself, the room is left grading a
  policy rather than choosing one.
- Does the scenario avoid the trap of making government look
  either omniscient or helpless? The best scenarios show
  institutions trying, partially succeeding, and running into
  genuine limits.

### The Storyteller

*Cares about: whether the narrative compels.*

- Is there a clear arc? Does something change between the first
  paragraph and the last — not just chronologically, but in the
  reader's understanding of what's at stake?
- Does the scenario have a turn — a moment where the world shifts,
  or a comfortable assumption breaks? Without one, it reads as
  description, not story.
- Is the ending earned? Does the closing tension follow from
  everything before it, or does it arrive out of nowhere?
- Is there texture — a vivid detail, a wry observation, a
  compressed phrase that sticks — or does it read like a briefing
  note?
- Does the scenario sustain one governing idea, or does it try to
  say everything and end up saying nothing?
