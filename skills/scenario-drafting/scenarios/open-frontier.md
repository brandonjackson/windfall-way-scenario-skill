# Open Frontier

> Scenario 2 from the GO-Science AI 2030 scenarios

AI systems are markedly more capable, though progress has slowed since 2026 and gains remain uneven – concentrated in domains with objective success criteria, like software engineering and cyber. Open-weight capabilities now match closed systems, with many companies and nations at the frontier. This presents broad economic opportunity and less US dependence, but also widespread malicious use and malfunctions – including fully automated cyberattacks and agents causing further harm by acting contrary to or evading operator intent. China has established itself as the leading open-weight provider, creating global infrastructural dependency.

AI spent its youth being trained by philosophers, poets, and scientists hired by well-capitalised American firms. But utopian model constitutions did not survive contact with the brutal price competition that began in late 2026. Freely available open-weight models delivered good-enough capability at the near-free cost of inference. Firm valuations crashed; data centres became stranded assets; and a race to the bottom on safety ushered in a new normal. The capability to automate was now accessible to all at low cost. But so too was the capability to disrupt society.

## Diffusion of Productivity

China quickly established itself as the leading open-weight provider, setting the technical standards and norms underpinning the world's digital infrastructure buildout. Open systems outperformed even expert humans in domains with clear success criteria like software engineering and mathematics. But they remained limited at open-ended and ambiguous tasks. That meant integrating them into existing organisations was harder than had been hoped. But the 40% of UK firms that had invested in adopting AI were pulling ahead, taking GDP growth with them to 2.5%, levels not seen in over a decade. The biggest success stories came from firms that built sophisticated AI workflows across their operations, cutting junior hires and redirecting capital into higher-value work.

## Diffusion of Threats

The same open-weight models were equally available to those with less productive intentions. The stories started appearing in local newspapers in 2027. A hospital trust was shut down by a cyberattack for a week. Three teenagers took their own lives in one week after sustained AI-generated bullying at school. A major high street chain closed permanently after an attack brought down its payment systems for a month. The Chinese open-weight models that underpinned much of the global AI infrastructure lacked the rigorous safety controls of their closed American counterparts. Safeguards could be stripped in hours, enabling automated attacks that were cheap, continuous, and self-replicating. Existing defences were no match. And there was no way to recall the models that enabled them.

## The New Normal

By 2029 the incidents were no longer local news. Automated cyberattacks hit critical infrastructure with the regularity of weather events, shaving points off GDP in the way that the JLR attack of 2025 had, but repeatedly. The NHS was paralysed by repeated strikes on its systems. A terrorist group, aided by an AI system, released a CBRN agent in a major UK city, killing twelve people. Attacks grew in sophistication. It was increasingly difficult to determine how attackers had gained access, or whether they were state-sponsored, state-tolerated, or simply criminal. The threat created opportunities. The UK AI sector pivoted to assurance and trustworthy AI as a competitive advantage, expanding by 25%. But the offence-defence balance was on a knife edge. One Friday in 2029, a cyberattack crippled a defence manufacturer. Firms across the supply chain spent the weekend assessing the damage. The underwriters at Lloyd's of London quietly exited the cyber insurance market on Monday morning. The economy proceeded at risk.

## Muddling Through

On the one hand, things weren't as bad as many had feared. Unemployment was just 5.5%. The first CBRN attack had not triggered a full pandemic. But daily life was getting worse in myriad little ways that added up. Online safety legislation struggled to keep up with the assaults on children's education and mental health. AI adoption in the NHS felt less like innovation and more like cost-cutting. Electricity bills inched upwards. And there was a growing sense that things were just beginning. This unease was weaponised by a new generation of persuasive agents designed to deepen political fragmentation. It was harder to know what was true, harder to know if an email was real, harder to trust that a system would be working tomorrow. Opinion didn't harden around a clear position. It fragmented.

## The Bind

By 2030 the bind was clear. Opposition to AI was growing in healthcare, education, and criminal justice. But so were the security bills—and AI was the only thing generating enough growth to cover them. The government could not afford to slow down. And it could not afford to keep going.
