# USA 2030: Paper Prosperity

By 2030 America looked better than ever—on paper. GDP growth was strong, unemployment remained reasonable, and headline productivity figures were the envy of the developed world. This scenario describes one possible future from the Hollow Boom quadrant.

This growth was powered by its world-leading AI sector. Early strategic investments meant that American AI systems were over a year ahead of their Chinese competitors. Yet what truly set the US apart was how quickly drop-in remote workers were adopted across the white-collar economy. Diffusion was faster than any previous general-purpose technology.

Corporate profits rose, public equities rallied, and valuations for AI-intensive firms reached extraordinary levels. Inflation eased in many AI-exposed services as the marginal cost of producing digital output fell. Even though disappointing physical capabilities limited physical output gains the United States appeared to have pulled off a historic supply-side acceleration.

One expansion in supply in particular had profound impacts. Abundant intelligence was rapidly reducing the scarcity value of human expertise. Professionals watched as their skills were commoditised. Employers realised that knowledge workers were increasingly interchangeable. This flexibility helped the labour market clear. Yet the direction of travel was unmistakable. Underemployment, gig work and precarity — long features of working-class American life — were finally reaching the upper tiers of the middle class.

Thus growth ran along a K-shaped curve determined overwhelmingly by asset ownership. Households that owned diversified financial assets, homes in appreciating metro areas, or shares in AI-intensive firms saw large gains. Households that relied primarily on wages benefited from cheaper digitized services, but faced increasing affordability pressures around scarce essentials: housing, healthcare, education, insurance, and in-person care. Fewer and fewer had jobs that could pay for a mortgage and send kids to college.

The response to this rising inequality from the American public couldn't have been clearer. They didn't want handouts. They just wanted good jobs that gave their kids a shot at the American Dream.
