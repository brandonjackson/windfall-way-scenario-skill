# The Incremental Path

> Scenario 1 from the Windfall Trust "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" workshop, September 2025, San Francisco. Created by Anna Yelizarova, Danny Buerkli, Jamais Cascio, and David Evan Harris.

AI hits technical barriers and remains primarily a tool to augment rather than replace workers. Occupations are reshaped, with many tasks automated and others redefined, while new roles and businesses emerge. In this scenario, the central divide is not between the employed and the unemployed, but between those able to harness AI effectively and those left behind. Much like the internet era, the gains may accrue less to a handful of dominant providers and more to the intermediaries and platforms that learn how to package AI into services people actually use.

## State of AI Tech

By 2035, the dream of "drop-in remote workers" that could seamlessly replace most human cognition has not materialized. Key limitations with real world environments, reasoning beyond narrow domains, as well as prohibitive energy and computational demands prevent exponential scaling. Systems plateau at impressive but fundamentally human-augmenting levels. Over the years, despite continued investment and research, many startups collapse under hype they could not deliver and major tech companies roll back products that were too costly or unreliable.

Even so, AI reshapes the landscape. It performs a growing range of tasks across industries but always requires a human hand to guide it. From content production to analysis, AI boosts productivity and fuels growth while simultaneously erasing many entry-level jobs. The displacement is often indirect: workers are not replaced by machines outright, but by other humans who know how to wield AI more effectively. Strategic roles that set vision and direction become more valuable: a single creative director can now oversee five times more output, with AI handling content production faster and cheaper than junior staff. As with the internet's impact on retail, AI disrupts some occupations while creating new ones. Innovation continues, businesses adapt, and new ventures thrive at lower margins. Entrepreneurial individuals also thrive, using cheap AI tools to spin up new ventures and compete with larger firms. But not everyone is wired to be an entrepreneur leaves many struggling to translate their contributions into economic security. We see lower costs for starting businesses, but higher skill requirements for employment – an uneven boom.

Like the utilities that delivered electricity, AI labs provide an essential but commoditized backbone. The real wealth accrues to the companies that build compelling applications and services on top of this infrastructure, creating new use cases that people are willing to pay for. As with electricity, no one got rich from the wires alone; prosperity flowed from the industries and products that learned how to harness it.

## Access to AI

Free on-device tools handle most casual needs, while $20/month subscriptions target professionals who demand higher reliability and features. For many, "good enough" assistants make paid tiers unnecessary. The same systems that drive inequality also democratize services: these advances help raise quality of life, even as economic gains concentrate among those who own or effectively leverage AI systems. Lower-end AI services are fairly interchangeable, leading to a situation where diverse populations rely on a fairly uniform set of information tools.

## Culture and Social Fabric

As AI-generated content floods daily life, artisanal goods, live performances, and personal services command premium prices, while some artists push back by making their work freely available, insisting authentic creativity should not be reserved for the wealthy. As AI-generated content tends to be more precisely targeted for audience tastes, human artists offering free material rarely dominate. The result is a two-tiered culture: the affluent access more human experiences, while everyone else relies increasingly on AI-mediated substitutes.

AI companions grow increasingly sophisticated, offering personalized interactions many find emotionally fulfilling. Meanwhile, birth and marriage rates continue their long decline, driven by economic uncertainty and eased by digital relationships that substitute for traditional bonds. AI does not cause these trends but reinforces them, providing convenient alternatives that reduce pressure to confront underlying social fragmentation.

## Governance Approaches

Competitive pressure prevents meaningful constraints on AI use. Early attempts at restrictive AI regulations quickly prove counterproductive as companies capture regulators or relocate to more permissive jurisdictions. Regulatory responses lag behind technological change and rely on existing legal frameworks and institutions.

## Education

Universities rush to integrate AI literacy into curricula as "AI fluency" becomes the new marker of professional competence, much like computer literacy in the 1990s. A stark generational divide emerges between digital natives who adapt quickly to human-AI collaboration and older workers who struggle with the transition. With entry-level roles disappearing, many find themselves unable to retrain or break into senior strategic positions, leaving a widening gap in the career ladder. Basic writing skills erode, as motivation for practice wanes when shortcuts are always available. Consequently, critical thinking skills degrade as well.
