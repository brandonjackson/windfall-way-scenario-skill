# Transformation Economy

> Scenario 4 from the GO-Science AI 2030 scenarios

AI progress has continued rapidly, with systems now able to automate almost any task performed by a remote human worker. Economic incentives have driven rapid adoption, displacing large segments of workers across white-collar sectors. Most systems are safe, and economic growth is unprecedented, however, inequality has soared, the influence of human workers is being increasingly marginalised as more decisions are automated, and the UK's technological dependency on the US has eroded its influence over global affairs.

Investments into responsible AI paid off. AI systems were increasingly thought of as humble and competent, on a level with dentists. First workers, then managers, and then directors began entrusting them with larger and larger briefs. By 2030 they could autonomously handle projects that took humans a month while only requiring a human to click approve twice. Delegating decision making to agentic systems had solved the UK's productivity challenge. But it had also displaced large parts of its middle class. Unemployment reached 17%.

## Capabilities

Drop-in remote workers arrived in 2028. This product enabled one US frontier lab to pull decisively ahead, commanding roughly 80% of the market for advanced AI. Its systems were not open. But they were safe and reliable enough to defuse calls for antimonopoly regulation. Competitors in China and elsewhere raced to catch up, but the capital and compute required had become prohibitive. The UK, without a frontier lab of its own, watched its dependency on US-controlled AI infrastructure deepen. AI agents excelled at writing legal briefs, building full financial models for business deals, designing and implementing software features end to end—the high-skill jobs that had powered 20th century social mobility.

## Rising Unemployment

The papers shrugged when AI began displacing entry-level white collar jobs. Everything changed as AI worked its way up the pyramid, automating the most complex parts of the professions: software engineering, law, accounting, finance, and the creative industries. Adoption was rapid. Large firms began to make deep cuts. Soon the middle rungs of the career ladder had vanished. It was unclear where it would stop. The media was filled with interviews of AI firm CEOs smiling saying "And just imagine - this is the worst these systems will ever be." The future of AI demand seemed certain. AI already consumed 20% of the grid and new data centres couldn't be built fast enough. Future demand for an educated workforce, however, was less clear. People who had followed the rules, earned degrees and built careers now found themselves competing for gig work with little hope of their old jobs coming back. Abundant intelligence had permanently devalued expertise.

## Hollow Growth

A new generation of AI-native firms achieved extraordinary productivity with tiny workforces. But for many established companies, the gains came from a simpler source: headcount reductions. GDP growth surged, hitting 7% in 2030. But it was a strange kind of prosperity. The gains flowed to asset owners and a thin layer of highly skilled workers. The price of professional services collapsed, benefiting those who could still afford them. But housing, energy, and childcare kept climbing. For most people, standards of living were falling even as the economy grew. The economy was running on a narrower and narrower base. And the high earners being displaced were also the consumers who had powered it. Their digital replacements didn't eat out, go shopping, or pay taxes. Consumption held up, but only because the new rich were spending.

## Fiscal Pressure

GDP was rising, yet much of the profit flowed back across the Atlantic, beyond the reach of HMRC. Corporation tax receipts were up, but in the UK it is mostly labour and consumption that are taxed, not capital. Historically the top 20% of earners had paid 75% of all income tax. As these earners were displaced, income tax and National Insurance receipts fell sharply. Yet welfare spending surged. The government's finances struggled to support existing services. Speeches in parliament heralding the next generation of good jobs were convincing in 2026. But capability increases in AI speechwriting proved insufficient to hide the growing contradictions of the transformation economy.

## Political Pressure

The newly displaced middle class did not find these contradictions difficult to interpret. An automated GP appointment was brilliant. But attempting to pay a mortgage on Universal Credit was less so. Things were getting worse. Nobody in charge seemed to have a plan. New political movements arose demanding an expansion of the safety net. Meanwhile, the bond markets began to express their view: that the UK needed to cut public spending to adjust to its new reality. Being highly automated, asset-rich, and fiscally poor.
