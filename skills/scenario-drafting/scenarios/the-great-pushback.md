# The Great Pushback

> Scenario 3 from the Windfall Trust "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" workshop, September 2025, San Francisco. Created by Anna Yelizarova, Danny Buerkli, Jamais Cascio, and David Evan Harris.

AI could replace many workers, but institutional friction and strong social backlash slow deployment. Societies make the choice to preserve human jobs through professional licensing, human-in-the-loop requirements or job guarantee schemes. This shapes the development of AI as an increasingly powerful tool that augments workers.

## State of AI Tech

By the 2030s, AI reached human-level performance across most cognitive tasks. Adoption, however, varied sharply across sectors. Professions like medicine, law, architecture, and engineering moved slowly, constrained by licensing rules, liability frameworks, and professional oversight that created natural barriers. Meanwhile, less regulated white-collar workers faced devastating displacement as AI rapidly transformed content creation, marketing, financial analysis, and customer service roles. Next came the cultural backlash as many found themselves displaced and unsupported.

AI can fully diagnose medical conditions, but medical boards mandate that licensed doctors must be present to "supervise" and take legal responsibility. AI can handle complete legal cases, but bar associations require attorneys to review and sign off on all AI work. AI can teach entire curricula, but teachers' unions secure laws ensuring human educators maintain classroom authority. The same AI that could replace an architect or radiologist is deliberately constrained by professional licensing, liability requirements, and new "human dignity" regulations born from mass protests.

Instead of "drop-in workers," with time, AI becomes an exoskeleton for human cognition.

## Access to AI

Consumer access to AI remains widespread, but its deployment is heavily regulated. Individuals can use AI for personal tasks, but businesses face strict requirements for human supervision in customer-facing and critical decision-making roles. This creates a bifurcated system where AI enhances human capabilities while preserving human agency and employment across sectors.

Certain designated special geographic zones operate under different rules: highly automated, minimally regulated environments where companies can deploy AI with few human workers. These become economically productive but socially barren spaces where few people choose to live. Human centred zones focus on an essential needs and services economy while these special economic zones focus on finance, trading, producing certain non-essential or luxury goods. People prefer "human-produced" marked products or content produced by human driven economies. Their needs are met so they see no appeal in buying cheaper AI goods.

## Culture and Social Fabric

A powerful coalition of workers, unions, and concerned citizens mobilized against AI. Mass protests outside tech company headquarters, coordinated strikes by knowledge workers, and viral social media campaigns demanding "human rights in the digital economy" force governments and professional associations to act decisively. This was done at an opportune time while humans were essential to the economy and had bargaining power. The rallying cry becomes clear: technology should serve humanity, not replace it.

But the backlash was not only about lost jobs. Many argued that AI systems were not designed to serve their users' needs, but rather to maximize corporate efficiency and capture attention. Lawsuits over the non-consenting use of copyrighted material in training data added legal weight to the cultural revolt. Concerns about privacy and surveillance intensified as AI tools harvested unprecedented amounts of behavioral data. Cultural resistance fused with economic anger, fueling mass protests and demands for systemic guardrails.

## Governance Approaches

Public backlash reshapes the political economy of AI. Professional licensing boards rapidly expand requirements for human oversight across industries and "human-in-the-loop" mandates. New "right to human review" legislation ensures people can demand human decision-makers in healthcare, legal, and educational contexts. Work-sharing mandates like 4-day work weeks redistribute available employment. Progressive taxation on AI-driven productivity gains funds expanded social programs and retraining initiatives. AI liability laws penalize the manufacturer for legal violations.

## Education

Societies make conscious decisions about which jobs to sacrifice and which to protect, while shepherding in an intentional, managed transition to new available work through educational programs. We are able to fill in needs in education and health care with a combination of humans moving into these professions and AI. The result is a labor market where more work emphasizes care, service, and direct human interaction, with AI filling in behind the scenes.
